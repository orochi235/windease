# The policy contract

Three of the library's decisions are replaceable, and they share one contract.

**Return a value to choose it, a deliberate negative to refuse, `undefined` to
defer to the built-in.**

| Policy | Where | Decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `acceptPolicy` | `<Container>`, `<Zone>`, `<Panel>` | whether a container takes a drop |
| `chooseSuccessor` | `new Store({ … })` | who takes focus when the focused node departs |
| `resolveNavigation` | `new Store({ … })` | how a direction resolves to a node |

The tri-state is the whole design. A consumer who wants to change one case does
not reimplement the rest — return `undefined` for everything else and the
built-in handles it.

Refusing and deferring are different. `null`/`false` is an answer; `undefined` is
"ask someone else".

## A bad policy degrades, it does not break

Each of these is arbitrary consumer code running inside a library operation. So:

**A policy that throws, or answers with something the library cannot use, is
traced and treated as `undefined`.**

The built-in decides, the gesture completes, and the failure shows up on a trace
category rather than as a broken drag. Only a returned *id* is validated —
`null` and `false` are deliberate answers and are never second-guessed.

A policy that calls back into the thing it replaces gets the built-in rather than
recursing forever.

## What is not a policy

`edgeScroll` is a tuning bag — nothing to refuse, nothing to defer to
([6.5](/?story=guide--6-drag-and-drop--6-5-edge-scroll)).

Locks are not policies either: a lock refuses regardless of what a policy said
([8.3](/?story=guide--8-customization--8-3-locks)).

`strategy.canAccept` and `strategy.navigate` are the *strategy's* opinion, which
a policy is asked ahead of.
